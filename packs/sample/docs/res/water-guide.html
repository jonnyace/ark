Making water safe to drink

This short guide is sample content written for Lastbook Ark. It summarises public guidance from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and Ready.gov. Always follow instructions from your local health department, which may differ.

When water may be unsafe

After floods, hurricanes, earthquakes or a broken water main, tap water may be contaminated with germs or chemicals. Listen for a boil-water notice from local officials. Do not use water that has a strong smell, an unusual colour or that may be contaminated with fuel or toxic chemicals; boiling and disinfecting will not make that water safe.

Boiling water

Boiling is the surest way to kill disease-causing germs, including viruses, bacteria and parasites. If the water is cloudy, let it settle, then filter it through a clean cloth, paper towel or coffee filter. Bring the clear water to a rolling boil for one minute. At elevations above 6,500 feet (about 2,000 metres), boil for three minutes. Let the boiled water cool, then store it in clean containers with covers.

Disinfecting with household bleach

If you cannot boil water, you can disinfect it with unscented household chlorine bleach. Check the label: use bleach that is meant for disinfection and has 6 or 8.25 percent sodium hypochlorite. For one gallon of clear water, add 8 drops of 6 percent bleach or 6 drops of 8.25 percent bleach. Double the amount if the water is cloudy. Stir the water and let it stand for at least 30 minutes before you use it. The water should have a slight chlorine smell; if it does not, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes.

Storing safe water

Store at least one gallon of water per person per day, for at least three days, for drinking and sanitation. Use clean, food-grade containers with tight caps, and keep them in a cool, dark place. Replace stored tap water every six months. Label each container with the date it was filled.
